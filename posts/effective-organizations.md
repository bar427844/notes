On ethics: I agree that reason matters in ethics and morality, and that it's partly objective. That is, there are some subjective preferences at the start, and then we use reason to work out what follows from them.

After long deliberation and reading, one rather arrives at the conclusion that a person has to submit to some authority. Deliberation is useful — it lets you choose a better authority.

Unfortunately, the authorities on offer don't exactly impress.

When it comes to careers, we have a few bloggers and 80k hours.

So what's the problem with 80k hours? OK, they recommend AI safety — OK, maybe that's fine. There are some jobs on the job board, some of them remote — hmm, I think so.

But hang on. We look at these organizations and something already doesn't sit right. Why is most of it in SF (San Francisco Bay Area)? Why are some not remote?

Then there's another list of things that don't sit right with me.

From my experience, nice, effective organizations hire on the basis of objective tests — competitions, ABCD tests, the matura, and so on. That seems to be missing in AI safety organizations.

An altruistic and effective organization — what comes to mind when one imagines one? Absolute effectiveness, rules that are a reflection of logic, the organization subordinated to human thought. And yet when we look at EA organizations, at AI safety ones, that isn't there.

Logically speaking, an effective organization should want to hire all people (literally all). Of course, at different pay — from a fraction of a cent a year to an SF salary. Naturally, voices appear saying that communication with an employee who works little, remotely, and inefficiently costs more than the employee. But that isn't true. We have software that can keep watch so that a "tier 1" employee (normal salary) doesn't spend more than X minutes (a year, a month, a week) communicating with an employee from a lower tier, where X can be arbitrarily small.

Of course, someone might argue that OpenAI, Anthropic, big businesses, and so on aren't like that. But instead of copying OpenAI (because the point isn't to make a second startup), one can wonder why they aren't like that.

Supposedly OpenAI hires in SF, but OpenAI's big success began with RLHF — that is, with hiring an army of remote contractors.

Could it be that their financial success rests on offering more flexible, comfortable remote work for low pay?

Maybe not, but how to make sense of it?

Isn't hiring in SF a selection of people who are egoists, who aren't attached to their family or their countries?

Maybe it's that people who are on site are easier to manipulate? That's good in business, but does a charity organization need it?
